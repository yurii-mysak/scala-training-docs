# CARL and STAR — Structuring Behavioural Answers

> **Priority:** Required
> **Est. time:** 45 min
> **Track:** Both
> **HelloInterview:** Behavioral — CARL Framework; Decode / Select / Deliver; Story Builder

Two frameworks are in circulation and you will meet both in prep material. HelloInterview teaches
**CARL**; nearly everything else teaches **STAR**. They are not competitors — they emphasise
different halves of a story, and Lyft's format rewards one of them noticeably more.

---

## 1 · The two frameworks side by side

| STAR | CARL | Difference |
|------|------|------------|
| **S**ituation — the setting | **C**ontext — the setting *and the stakes* | CARL folds "why this mattered" into the opening; STAR often leaves stakes implicit. |
| **T**ask — what you were asked to do | *(absorbed into Context)* | STAR's Task step is where passive framing creeps in ("I was assigned..."). CARL removes the slot entirely. |
| **A**ction — what you did | **A**ctions — what you did | Same, but CARL's plural is deliberate: multiple decisions, not one heroic move. |
| **R**esult — the outcome | **R**esults — the outcome | Same. |
| *(none)* | **L**earnings — what changed in you or the org | The differentiator. This is where Staff signal lives. |

### 1.1 Why the L matters at Lyft

Two of Lyft's three values are satisfied mostly in the L:

- **Be yourself** wants self-awareness — that is a Learning.
- **Uplift others** wants the change to have outlived you — that is a Learning applied to the org.

A STAR answer that stops at Result answers the question and scores mid. The interviewer then has to
spend a follow-up extracting the learning, which costs you time in a 45-minute round with 6-9
questions. Put it in the answer.

### 1.2 The Task trap

STAR's **T** invites: *"I was tasked with migrating the service."* That single clause is a Senior
tell — it puts the origin of the work outside you. CARL has no Task slot, which is one practical
reason to default to it. If you use STAR anyway, convert Task into a decision:
*"I decided the migration had to happen before the next quarter's load, and I made that case to the
platform group."* See [senior-vs-staff-framing.md](senior-vs-staff-framing.md).

## 2 · Which to use when

| Question type | Use | Reason |
|---------------|-----|--------|
| Failure, bug you caused, decision you reversed | **CARL** | The Learnings slot is the entire point of the question. |
| Mentoring, inclusion, persuading a peer | **CARL** | Uplift-others questions are scored on what persisted afterwards. |
| Proud project, impact, "make it happen" | **STAR-with-L** | Results carry the weight; keep Actions crisp and finish with one Learning line. |
| "Walk me through your background" | Neither | That is a narrative, not a story. See [transition-narrative.md](transition-narrative.md). |
| Hypothetical ("what would you do if...") | Neither | Answer with a principle plus one real precedent, compressed. |

Default to CARL. Treat STAR as CARL with the L truncated when the question is purely about outcome
and you are short on time.

## 3 · The 90-second version

Lyft's format is breadth over depth: many short stories with heavy follow-ups. Your **opening answer
is 90 seconds**, not five minutes. That is roughly 200-230 spoken words. Budget:

| Slot | Seconds | Words | Content |
|------|---------|-------|---------|
| Context | 20 | ~45 | System, scale, stakes, and your position in the org. One sentence each. |
| Actions | 40 | ~100 | Two or three decisions, not a chronology. Each with the alternative you rejected. |
| Results | 20 | ~45 | One primary number with a before/after and a time window. |
| Learnings | 10 | ~25 | One sentence. What you now do differently, or what the org kept. |

### 3.1 Compression rules

- **Cut the chronology.** "First we did X, then Y, then Z" is the main cause of overrun. Replace with
  the two or three decisions that actually mattered.
- **Cut supporting cast you will not name again.** If a person appears once and never returns, drop them.
- **Cut the technology tour.** Name at most two technologies in the opening. The follow-up will ask.
- **Keep every number.** Numbers are the cheapest signal per second in the whole answer.
- **Keep the cost.** A trade-off that cost nothing is not a trade-off. One clause: "which cost us
  two weeks of migration tooling we then threw away."

### 3.2 The headroom principle

Deliberately leave the two most interesting details *out* of the 90 seconds. The interviewer's
follow-up then lands on ground you have already prepared, and the conversation feels like discovery
rather than recital. Candidates who front-load everything get follow-ups into territory they have
not thought about.

## 4 · The 5-minute version

When an interviewer keeps pulling — "tell me more about that", "how did you decide", "what did the
other team say" — the story must expand without repeating. Expansion is not slower delivery; it is
**four extra layers** you can add in any order.

| Layer | What it adds | Typical trigger |
|-------|--------------|-----------------|
| **Alternatives** | The two designs you rejected and the specific reason each failed | "Why that approach?" |
| **Opposition** | Who disagreed, what their strongest argument was, what you conceded | "Did everyone agree?" |
| **Mechanism** | How the measurement worked, how the rollout was staged, what the failure mode was | "How did you measure?" |
| **Aftermath** | What happened 6-12 months later, who owns it now, what broke since | "What happened after you left?" |

A 90-second core plus any two layers is roughly 3 minutes; all four is roughly 5. Prepare the layers
as bullet points, not prose — prose gets memorised and memorised prose sounds rehearsed.

### 4.1 Expansion discipline

- Add a layer **once per prompt**. Dumping all four on the first "tell me more" wastes the round's
  budget and reads as poor calibration.
- If you have exhausted the layers, say so: *"That's about as deep as my memory goes on that one —
  happy to go into the follow-on project instead."* Honest termination beats invention, and it hands
  the interviewer control back, which they will read as good judgement.

## 5 · Signalling structure without sounding scripted

Structure should be **audible but not announced**. The interviewer needs to know where they are in
the story; they must not hear a framework.

| Do not say | Say instead |
|-----------|-------------|
| "The situation was..." | "This was the mortgage-origination platform, about 40 engineers around it." |
| "My task was to..." | "The thing that had to change was..." / "I decided..." |
| "The actions I took were..." | "Two decisions mattered here." |
| "The result was..." | "By the end of the quarter..." |
| "What I learned was..." | "What I do differently now is..." / "The team still runs..." |

### 5.1 Signposting phrases that work

- *"Two decisions mattered here."* — tells the listener to expect exactly two, and lets them track you.
- *"The part I got wrong first was..."* — signals a Learning is coming, and buys credibility early.
- *"Numbers, if it's useful:"* — offers the metric without lecturing. Especially good with a PM
  interviewer who may not want the metric yet.
- *"The trade-off was X for Y, and X cost us..."* — compresses an entire design discussion.

### 5.2 Anti-scripting techniques

- Vary the entry point. Some stories should open on the number, some on the conflict, some on the
  mistake. If all twelve open with "So, the context was...", the pattern is audible by story four.
- Rehearse from **bullets, not paragraphs**. See [mock-rehearsal-plan.md](mock-rehearsal-plan.md).
- Leave one deliberate mid-story correction: *"— actually it was two quarters, not one."* Natural
  speech self-corrects; perfectly linear delivery does not.
- Never recite a value name out loud. "This shows how I uplift others" is the single most damaging
  sentence available to you.

## 6 · Handling Lyft's follow-up drilling

Expect two to four probes per answer. They come in recognisable families.

| Probe family | Example | How to answer |
|--------------|---------|---------------|
| **Ownership split** | "What was your role?" / "What did you personally do?" | Name the decision you owned and, separately, what others owned. Claiming the whole thing is a bigger risk than claiming too little. |
| **Measurement** | "How did you measure?" | Name the instrument, then the number, then the confidence. See [metrics-for-stories.md](metrics-for-stories.md). |
| **Counterfactual** | "What would you do differently?" | Give a real alternative you now prefer, with the reason. "Nothing" is a failed answer. |
| **Opposition** | "Did anyone disagree?" | Always yes. If a design had no opposition it was not consequential. Name the strongest counter-argument fairly. |
| **Scale** | "How big was the team / the traffic / the blast radius?" | Have these numbers ready for all twelve stories. Not knowing your own system's scale is a Staff-level red flag. |
| **Aftermath** | "Is it still running?" | Honesty including "it was decommissioned" — with why. |

### 6.1 The four-probe rule

Prepare each story to survive **four consecutive probes**. Practically: write four plausible probes
per story in [story-bank.md](story-bank.md) and answer them aloud. The reported Lyft round asked
three consecutive follow-ups on a single opening question, so this is the realistic depth, not a
worst case.

### 6.2 When a probe lands on something you do not know

Use this order, and never skip step 1:

1. Say what you do not know: *"I don't have the exact p99 in front of me."*
2. Give the shape you are confident about: *"It was single-digit-hundreds of milliseconds, and the
   change was roughly a halving."*
3. Say how you would get it: *"It was on a Grafana board off the service's Prometheus metrics."*

This scores better than a confident wrong number, and it is also the honest answer.

## Interview questions

**1. Recent project you're proud of. What was your role? What was impact? How did you measure?** **[Reported at Lyft]**
Deliver the 90-second CARL core, holding two details back. Answer "role" as the decision you owned
plus what others owned. Answer "impact" with one primary number and a time window. Answer "how did
you measure" with the instrument first, the number second, the confidence third.

**2. Tell me about a failure.** **[Reported at Lyft]**
Use CARL specifically because of the L. Context includes what you believed and why it was reasonable.
Actions include the decision that turned out wrong. Results state the real cost without softening.
Learnings name the mechanism you changed, and whether it stuck.

**3. Did you convince your colleague about your solution, and how?** **[Reported at Lyft]**
Expand the Actions slot into the persuasion mechanism: the artefact you built to make the argument
checkable, what you conceded, and how the decision was finally made. Add the Opposition layer
unprompted — it is the substance of the question.

**4. Can you give me a shorter version of that?**
A real and common interrupt. It means you overran. Restart from the number and the decision:
"Short version — we were losing X, I changed Y, it went to Z." Do not apologise at length; the
recovery is the signal being tested.

**5. That sounds like a team effort — what did you personally do?**
Do not retreat into smaller claims. Name the specific decision, the specific argument you made, and
the specific thing that would not have happened without you. Then name what your colleagues owned,
by role. Precision here is the difference between honest and hedging.

**6. What would you do differently?**
Give one concrete alternative and why you now prefer it. If the honest answer is that the decision
was right, say what you would do differently in *execution* — the rollout, the comms, the sequencing —
rather than claiming nothing.

**7. You've mentioned that project a few times — do you have another example?**
A signal you are over-indexing on one story. Switch immediately to a different slot in the bank,
ideally from a different employer and a different value. This is why twelve slots exist.

**8. Walk me through how you'd structure that story if you had thirty seconds.**
Rare but occasionally asked by bar raisers as a communication probe. Answer: problem, decision,
number. One sentence each, no context, no names.
